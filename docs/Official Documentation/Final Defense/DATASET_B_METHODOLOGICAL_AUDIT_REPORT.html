1. Section 3.8.3 Generation Methodology for Operational Dataset B (Page 49–50)

Why: Fulfills Directive 1 & 2 by Assoc. Prof. Vista to document the exact Python simulation module, rules, and parameters.

### 3.8.3 Generation Methodology for Operational Dataset B To establish an operational predictive model aligned with service-completion timelines rather than customer claiming behavior, Dataset B was developed as a 10,000-record simulated operational dataset. Dataset B models the operational duration from order intake to completion (Received-to-Ready-for-Release Duration) based on the authorized business rules and service configurations of Shoelotskey Villamor-Pasay. The dataset generation procedure was implemented in Python using a deterministic pseudo-random number generator (NumPy PCG64, fixed seed = 20261002). The generator utilized an empirical bootstrap design drawing from 35 collision-free canonical job templates derived from Shoelotskey’s 119 validated historical orders. In accordance with historical business patterns, job templates were sampled with replacement based on empirical historical frequencies from the 119 historical orders (for example, single-pair Basic Cleaning accounts for 46.22% of historical orders). Candidate intake dates were sampled with equal probability from a 305-day operational calendar (Monday through Saturday, excluding Sundays and eight statutory holidays in 2025). Service turnaround duration was calculated using Shoelotskey’s verified non-additive base tier rules: 10 days for Basic Cleaning and Minor Reglue; 20 days for Unyellowing and Minor Restoration; and 25 days for Full Reglue and Color Renewal. Orders eligible for rush processing (single-service Basic Cleaning of three pairs or fewer) were assigned a 1-day turnaround. Operational calendar rollovers were applied deterministically: if an order's target completion date fell on a Sunday or statutory shop closure, the Ready-for-Release date was automatically rolled over to the next open business day, introducing calendar-driven turnaround extensions of one to five days across 24.0% of the dataset. Exactly zero Gaussian noise or artificial delay was injected. The resulting 10,000 records were partitioned into 7,000 training records (70%), 1,500 validation records (15%), and 1,500 sealed final test records (15%). Feature Set C was restricted to 12 operational predictors known at intake, intentionally excluding target-revealing variables such as rule base days, raw completion dates, and rollover indicators to prevent target leakage.

2. Narrative Below Table 4: Split-Ratio Sensitivity & Retention Justification (Page 50)

Why: Addresses verbal directive to evaluate split ratios and justifies keeping 70/15/15 without claiming it is universally optimal.

To assess sensitivity to dataset partitioning, alternative split ratios (60/20, 65/15, 70/15, 75/15, and 80/10 equivalents) were evaluated across 5 random seeds using development records. Across all candidate configurations, mean validation MAE remained tightly bounded between 0.0668 and 0.0701 days (a narrow variance of ~4.7 minutes). While the 80/10 equivalent achieved a slightly lower mean validation MAE (0.0668 days vs. 0.0693 days, a difference of ~3.6 minutes), the 70/15/15 partition was retained based on: (1) bit-level reproducibility with frozen defense baseline artifacts; (2) adequate sample allocation, providing 1,500 validation and 1,500 test records (50% more test cases than a 1,000-record split) to ensure robust statistical power across all 35 canonical templates and rare multi-day calendar rollover events; (3) lower mean RMSE (0.3609 days vs. 0.3612 days) and tighter stability across random seeds (±0.0083 days vs. ±0.0116 days); and (4) preservation of the sealed test protocol without post-hoc data reshuffling. The 70/15/15 split is therefore retained as a methodologically sound and stable configuration rather than claimed as universally optimal.

4. Narrative for Table 6: Model Selection Rationale (Page 51–52)

Why: Reconciles Decision Tree lower MAE vs Random Forest lower RMSE without unsupported catastrophic claims.

Three regression algorithms were evaluated using the 1,500-record validation partition: Linear Regression, Decision Tree Regressor, and Random Forest Regressor. Model selection was not decided on the basis of MAE alone. Although the Decision Tree achieved a slightly lower validation MAE (0.0616 days compared with 0.0641 days for Random Forest, a difference of approximately 3.6 minutes), Random Forest produced the superior RMSE (0.2994 days vs. 0.3093 days), higher R² (0.9987 vs. 0.9986), and higher ±1-day tolerance accuracy (97.73% vs. 97.53%). The lower RMSE indicates that Random Forest was more effective at minimizing larger prediction errors on complex multi-service and holiday rollover cases. Furthermore, aggregating 300 decorrelated decision trees provided greater ensemble stability and variance reduction compared with a single decision tree. Based on combined evaluation measures and ensemble robustness, Random Forest was selected as the finalized operational model.

1. Section 4.3 Narrative: Finalized Model Performance (Page 153–155)

Why: Reports frozen test results and explains repeated simulated configurations vs real-world validation.

As detailed in the Machine Learning Development section, Dataset B contained 10,000 simulated operational service orders divided into 7,000 training, 1,500 validation, and 1,500 final test records. The final test set remained untouched throughout feature engineering and hyperparameter optimization and was evaluated once after freezing the selected Random Forest model. Candidate model validation on the 1,500-record validation set showed that Linear Regression obtained an MAE of 2.4764 days, Decision Tree obtained 0.0616 days, and Random Forest obtained 0.0641 days. Although Decision Tree produced a slightly lower MAE, Random Forest obtained a lower RMSE (0.2994 days vs. 0.3093 days), higher R² (0.9987), and higher ±1-day accuracy (97.73%), supporting its selection. On the independent 1,500-record final test partition, the finalized Random Forest model achieved an MAE of 0.0815 days (approximately 1.96 hours average error), an RMSE of 0.4042 days, and an R² of 0.9976. Tolerance analysis showed that 1,458 predictions (97.20%) were within ±1 day of the synthetic target value, 98.93% within ±2 days, 99.53% within ±3 days, and 100.00% within ±5 days. Feature vector overlap analysis revealed that 81.91% of validation vectors (679 / 829) and 80.72% of test vectors (695 / 861) appeared in the training set. This high overlap is characteristic of craft shop operations where customer transactions naturally cluster around 35 discrete service templates, 6 working weekdays, and 12 intake months across 2,237 unique operational profiles. The strong test performance demonstrates that the Random Forest has faithfully reconstructed the underlying business rules and calendar rollover logic across repeated operational configurations. However, because Dataset B consists of simulated operational orders, these results measure rule-learning fidelity within a controlled environment and should not be interpreted as equivalent to external validation on actual future footwear orders.

2. Residual Formula & Bias Interpretation (Page 155–156)

Why: Fixes the formula label to "Synthetic Target Value" and correctly explains signed error bias.

Residual error on the final test partition was calculated as: Residual = Synthetic Ready-for-Release Target Value – Predicted Duration Under this definition, a positive residual indicates underprediction (the target duration exceeded the model estimate), while a negative residual indicates overprediction (the model estimate exceeded the target duration). Across the 1,500 final test records, the overall mean signed residual was +0.0018 days (approximately 2.6 minutes), indicating that predictions were exceptionally well-balanced with virtually zero systematic directional bias. Across non-rollover orders (N = 1,260), test MAE was 0.0474 days with 98.41% within ±1 day; across rollover orders (N = 240), test MAE was 0.2610 days with 90.83% within ±1 day, confirming that residual error is concentrated on multi-day holiday rollover boundaries.

2. Predictive Performance of the Random Forest Regressor The machine learning component was developed and evaluated through two distinct stages involving Dataset A and Dataset B. Prediction tolerance analysis showed that 97.20% of predictions were within ±1 day, 98.93% within ±2 days, 99.53% within ±3 days, and 100.00% within ±5 days. The model was integrated into the operational system to provide an active ML Predicted Release Date for staff planning, while official customer commitments remain governed by rule-based Standard Release Dates until sufficient real-world completion timestamps are accumulated.

1.2 Algorithmic Pipeline & Random Seed

Dataset B was synthesized using an empirical canonical bootstrap generator designed to emulate Shoelotskey's operational order intake while enforcing verified shop business rules: 

# generate_dataset_b.py: Line 158
rng = np.random.default_rng(seed=20261002)

The script initializes NumPy's 64-bit PCG (Permuted Congruential Generator) using a fixed integer seed: Seed = 20261002. The sampling pipeline proceeds through three formal stages:

  1. Operational Calendar Initialization (Lines 54–63): A reference calendar of all open working days in year 2025 (Monday through Saturday, excluding statutory holidays and special closures) was constructed, resulting in exactly 305 eligible intake dates.
  2. Canonical Template Derivation (Lines 69–140; config.json: lines 193–1024): All 119 validated historical orders of Shoelotskey were collapsed into 35 collision-free canonical job templates (CT01 to CT35) capturing the exact joint empirical distribution of pair counts and service types.
  3. Bootstrap Sampling (Lines 161–162): The generator drew 10,000 independent samples with replacement from the canonical template pool and eligible intake dates using the deterministic random number generator: 
    sampled_indices = rng.choice(len(canonical_pool), size=n_samples, replace=True)
sampled_intake_indices = rng.choice(len(candidate_intake_dates), size=n_samples, replace=True)
    Because canonical_pool contains all 119 historical orders from Dataset A, canonical templates were sampled according to empirical historical frequencies (Probability = Historical Template Count / 119 orders), not uniform probabilities (1 in 35, or 2.86%). For example, CT01 (Basic Cleaning 1 pair) constitutes 46.22% (55/119) of sampling probability, whereas rare multi-service templates (CT08–CT35) constitute 0.84% (1/119) each. In contrast, candidate intake dates (305 open days) were sampled with uniform probability (equal probability of 1 in 305).

### 3.8.3 Generation Methodology for Operational Dataset B
To establish an operational predictive model aligned with service-completion timelines rather than customer claiming behavior, Dataset B was developed as a 10,000-record simulated operational dataset.
